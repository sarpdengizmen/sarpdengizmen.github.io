MIDI Robot

MIDI is a modular mobile robot platform built as a Capstone project for METU's ME462 course. The name stands for the design philosophy at its core: Modular, Integrated, Durable, and Improvable. The platform was designed not as a one-off demonstration piece, but as a foundation — a robot that anyone from a curious first-year student to a PhD researcher can pick up, extend, and use.

The team — Ege Sarp Dengizmen, Erdem Bayraktar, Muhammed Ömer Yiğit, Omar Habib, and Seçkin Eren Yetim — designed and manufactured MIDI with access to METU's ROMER and Micromanufacturing laboratories.

Mechanical Platform

MIDI is designed to carry payloads up to 15 kg without structural compromise. The chassis uses a stacked-floor architecture — additional sensor floors can be bolted on without modifying the base structure. The four-corner bumper system acts both as collision sensors and as mechanical springs, absorbing low-speed impacts that would otherwise damage the chassis or sensors.

Manufacturing was kept intentional: every bracket, mount, and structural member was designed for the specific capabilities of the METU Micromanufacturing Lab — laser cutting, CNC milling, and FDM printing. The goal was a robot that could be reproduced by any reasonably equipped university lab.

Sensor Suite

The base configuration ships with a comprehensive sensor suite for indoor navigation and research:

  • LiDAR — 360° planar scan for obstacle avoidance and SLAM
  • Wide-angle camera — visual perception and object detection
  • IMU — attitude and acceleration data for state estimation
  • 4× Ultrasonic sensors — short-range obstacle detection at the chassis corners
  • 4× Bumper switches — contact detection for collision recovery

Sensors are mounted on standardised rails that accept custom brackets, making it straightforward to add or reposition sensors as mission requirements change.

Power System

MIDI runs on an 18 V Makita battery — chosen for its wide availability, hot-swap capability, and the fact that battery packs and chargers are already present in most university workshops. A dedicated UPS module protects the Raspberry Pi from voltage sags and sudden disconnection, preventing filesystem corruption during hard shutdowns. Power is distributed through a fused terminal block to each subsystem independently.

Software and Programmability

The onboard computer is a Raspberry Pi running ROS 2, with Python as the primary user-facing language. The architecture was deliberately chosen to lower the barrier to entry: a student who knows basic Python can write a behaviour, test it in simulation, and deploy it on real hardware within a single lab session. For expert users, the same platform supports C++ nodes, custom firmware on the motor drivers, and integration with external GPU compute for vision-intensive tasks.
